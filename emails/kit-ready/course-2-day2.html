Better at school, worse at life
{{ message_content }}

Yesterday was the definition. Today is why it matters, and why I think it matters more now than when we were kids.

I taught for fifteen years. Somewhere around 2020 I started noticing something that worried me more than any test score. The kids were getting better at school and worse at life.

They could fill in a worksheet. But hand them a task without step-by-step instructions and they’d wait for someone to tell them what to do. Get one answer wrong and stop trying. Fall apart when the schedule changed.

One moment stays with me. A grade 4 class, independent work time, and one boy wasn’t writing. By the time I noticed, five or maybe ten minutes had gone by.

He didn’t have a pencil.

There was a basket of extra pencils on my desk and a room full of kids who had one. He didn’t try either. He waited for an adult to notice.

That isn’t a bad kid. That’s a kid who had learned that being stuck means waiting, because someone always comes. And I’ll be honest: when I went home, I saw the same thing at my own table. I did a lot for my kids. Mostly out of love, sometimes just because it was faster.

What I think happened

Learning got separated from real life, and it shows up in two ways.

Kids stopped being able to do things
Because they rarely get to practise.
Kids stopped wanting to learn
Because when learning isn’t about anything real, it’s boring.
Why real tasks stick

Kids remember what they did and forget what they were told. Our kitchen faucet needed replacing, and my daughter, who’s ten, asked if she could be the one to put it in. The fast answer was later. Her dad said yes, and it took an hour instead of twenty minutes. She did everything she could, and he did the two parts she wasn’t strong enough for. Next time it breaks, she’s the one who knows how.

The research agrees, and so does what parents tell researchers.

1.5×
In 2014, a review of 225 studies in university science and math classes found that students who only listened to lectures were one and a half times more likely to fail than students who actually did the work.
4 in 5
parents of 9 to 11 year olds say free time without an adult is good for kids. Only half would let that same kid find something in another aisle of the store. (University of Michigan Mott Poll, 2023)

That first one is adults at university. Imagine what it means for a ten year old.

Why it matters more now

A computer can write the essay now, solve the equation and summarise the chapter in seconds. So think about what’s left for a person to do.

What’s left for a person to do
Deciding what’s worth doingNoticing when something is offTalking to a real person and working it outPlanning a dayKeeping going when the first try doesn’t work

None of that comes from a worksheet. All of it comes from doing real things. And our kids get less room to practise than we did. Nobody is doing anything wrong. The day just isn’t set up for kids to practise.

So I took a year off, unpaid, and travelled with my two. No curriculum. We learned by doing: budgets in another currency, maps, talking to strangers, figuring it out when things went wrong. They came home more capable and more themselves, and I never went back.

Try this today
When your kid gets stuck on something small today, wait ten seconds before you help. Just ten. Watch what they do with them.
